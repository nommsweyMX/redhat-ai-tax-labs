SLIDE 02 · WHY NOW

The work is seasonal, textual and unforgiving

3–12 min block begins
On screenDemand curve — April at 6× the August low, far above the flat staffed-capacity line — plus four cards ending on the red one: "the data cannot leave."

Do not lead with the technology. Lead with the shape of the problem: seasonal demand spikes against a fixed workforce, decades of correspondence logic locked in code nobody wants to touch, and data spread across systems that were never designed to talk.

The chart is the argument: a year of demand arrives in six weeks, far above any capacity you can justify staffing year-round. The shaded gap is what automation absorbs.

AI is interesting here precisely because it works on unstructured language — which is most of what a tax agency actually handles. And the last card decides the architecture: federal tax information cannot go to a public endpoint. The model has to come to the data.

Pivot lineChoosing a model is a six-month decision. Choosing where models run, how they are governed and how they scale is a ten-year one. If they accept that sentence, everything after it follows.
NEXT → four different jobs get called AI
SLIDE 03 · AI IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Four different jobs get called “AI”

On screenFour cards — Predictive (estimate), Generative/LLM (create), Agentic (reason + act), Automation (execute) — with analogies, and a dashed "what it is not" box.

Start with the umbrella. Federal audiences hear "AI" used as if every technique is the same. It is not. Predictive AI estimates or classifies. Generative AI creates. An LLM is a generative model specialized in language. Agentic systems add tools and workflow. Automation executes known steps.

Walk the analogies — the weather forecast, the well-read drafting assistant, the junior analyst with a restricted tool belt, the checklist that performs the checklist (that is where Ansible belongs).

The strongest mission pattern combines them rather than asking one model to do everything.

Land the not-boxPredictive AI is not a crystal ball. An LLM is not a database, a policy authority, or a source of truth. An agent is not permission to bypass controls.
NEXT → inside an LLM (the stack)
SLIDE 04 · INSIDE AN LLM

An LLM is the engine — not the whole vehicle

On screenSix stacked layers (mission app → context/RAG → model → inference → platform → compute + data) and the "three knobs people confuse" box.

Explain the layers from the bottom up. The GPU is compute. Inference is the running engine — vLLM, the Red Hat AI Inference Server. The model contains learned statistical patterns — not a live copy of IRS policy. Context is the open book we hand it for this request. RAG retrieves relevant, approved material into that context. The mission app sits on top and asks for help.

Then the three knobs: prompting is better instructions for this request; RAG is the right reference material for this request; fine-tuning changes learned behavior for future requests. Use fine-tuning for durable behavior — not for facts that change every filing season.

NEXT → data to judgement (the staircase)
SLIDE 05 · DATA TO JUDGEMENT

From data to judgement — and who climbs each step

On screenFour-step staircase: Data → Information → Knowledge → Judgement. Pills on the risers: predictive AI, generative AI + RAG, people. The top step is marked "stays human."

Walk the stairs left to right. Each step up is worth more to the mission and harder to automate honestly. Predictive models climb the first riser — score, match, flag. Generative models with retrieval climb the second — read, ground, draft. The top step is deliberately human — a determination someone signs.

The backlog lives at the bottom of the ladder: transcribing, matching, routing, summarizing. Every hour a machine absorbs down there is an hour an examiner spends deciding — the only work that resolves a case.

Judgement is never automated by design — that rule is what makes the deployment defensible to counsel and oversight.

If the room remembers one pictureMake it this one.
NEXT → predictive vs generative (the governance table)
SLIDE 06 · TWO KINDS OF AI

Predictive and generative AI answer different questions

On screenFive-row comparison table (learns from / produces / in tax terms / fails as / governed by) plus two cards: "they compose" and "one platform runs both."

Most rooms use one word — AI — for two different tools, and most failed projects picked the wrong one. One produces a number from structured history; the other produces language.

The row that matters is how they fail: a predictive model fails as a bad number, visible in precision and recall. A generative model fails as a plausible sentence — which is why grounding and human review are non-negotiable. They fail differently, so they are governed differently.

Close on composition: a risk score picks which case deserves attention, a language model prepares the brief and the draft, and a person decides. One cluster serves both, side by side — same GPUs, same registry, same audit trail.

SLIDE 07 · WHERE AI LANDS

Where AI actually lands in the filing lifecycle

12–18 min block begins
On screenSix lifecycle stages. Five carry a red "human in the loop" pill; exam selection is greyed with "governance first."

Walk left to right. The highlighted stages are where language models earn their keep first — high-volume, text-heavy, and a human reviewer is already in the loop, which makes them safe places to start.

Exam selection is deliberately NOT highlighted. That is a determination affecting a taxpayer, and it needs a much heavier governance conversation. Saying this unprompted buys enormous credibility.

Two rules of thumb: start where a human already reviews the output — if a person signs the letter today, a model that drafts it changes throughput without changing accountability. And automate the toil around the model, not just the model — provisioning, patching, scaling and evidence are most of the work, and that problem is already solved.

NEXT → the operations loop
SLIDE 13 · AI FOR OPERATIONS

The useful pattern is a loop, not a chatbot

24–28 min block begins
On screenThree flywheel steps — predict, explain, ground + act — the knowledge-flywheel bar, and the "why is this service slow?" example.

Use the concrete story: predictive AI sees telemetry patterns and flags elevated failure risk. Generative AI summarizes the evidence and explains the likely issue in language. RAG pulls the approved troubleshooting and operations knowledge. Ansible performs only the remediations that policy permits.

Then the flywheel: after review, the incident summary, root cause and successful remediation become searchable operations knowledge for the next event.

The key boundary: generated text does not become truth automatically. A human or workflow gate curates it first. The LLM can propose a root cause; telemetry, retrieved sources and deterministic checks establish whether it is actually supported. Do not let fluency become authority.

Ninety days, then ninety more

LAB 04 · RUN THE REFUSAL IF HALLUCINATION COMES UP

Ground answers in your own guidance

OpenShift AI · vector store · Llama Stack~5 minyou@workbench:~/rag
Goal, said out loudA model that sounds confident and cites nothing is a liability in a tax agency. Put the published guidance behind the model so every answer returns the passage it came from.

llama-stack-client inference chat-completion --message "What is the penalty rate for late filing in the 2031 tax year?"
