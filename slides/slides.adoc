== Four different jobs get called “AI”

`Rung: information · knowledge · judgement`

Treat AI as a toolbox, not a magic employee. Pick the tool by the job you need done.

[cols="1,3,2", options="header"]
|===
| Term | What it actually does | Analogy

| *Predictive AI* — estimate (data → information)
| Finds patterns in historical data to score, classify or forecast an outcome.
| A weather forecast: “there is a 70% chance,” not a guarantee.

| *Generative AI / LLM* — create (information → knowledge)
| Produces new language by predicting useful next tokens from patterns learned during training and context supplied now.
| A very well-read drafting assistant — fast, fluent, and still capable of being wrong.

| *Agentic AI* — reason + act (knowledge → action)
| Pairs a model with approved tools, state and rules so it can plan steps and call systems.
| A junior analyst with a badge and a restricted tool belt — permissions still matter.

| *Automation* — execute (executes judgement)
| Runs deterministic, tested steps when a condition or human decision says to act.
| A checklist that actually performs the checklist. This is where Ansible belongs.
|===

Predictive AI is *not* a crystal ball. An LLM is *not* a database, policy
authority or source of truth. An agent is *not* permission to bypass controls.

[.notes]
--
Start with the umbrella. Federal audiences hear AI used as if every technique
is the same. It is not. Predictive AI estimates or classifies. Generative AI
creates. An LLM is a generative model specialized in language. Agentic systems
add tools and workflow. Automation executes known steps.

The strongest mission pattern combines them rather than asking one model to do
everything.

Land the on-ramp line: if you are not automated, you are not going to have a
good generative or predictive AI inside your boundary — automation is the
prerequisite.

== The work is seasonal, textual and unforgiving

`Rung: data`

[cols="1,2,3"]
|===
| *Demand* | Peak load, fixed staff | Idle capacity in August is unjustifiable; exhausted capacity in April is a headline. A year of demand arrives in six weeks — April is 6× the August low.
| *Systems* | Rules locked in legacy code | Rewriting the systems of record is a decade. Wrapping them in intelligent workflows is a quarter.
| *Data* | The answers are in the text | Correspondence, transcripts and case notes are unstructured — exactly what language models are built for.
| *The constraint that decides everything* | The data cannot leave (data — it stays) | FTI handling rules rule out public endpoints. The model comes to the data — on premises, accredited cloud, or air-gapped enclave.
|===

[.notes]
--
Do not lead with the technology. Lead with the shape of the problem: seasonal
demand spikes against a fixed workforce, decades of correspondence logic locked
in code nobody wants to touch, and data spread across systems that were never
designed to talk.

AI is interesting here precisely because it works on unstructured language —
which is most of what a tax agency actually handles.

Context to use with tact: large federal accounts are losing institutional
knowledge as people age out or are RIF'd — capturing what the workforce knows
is part of why this matters now.

The chart: a year of demand arrives in six weeks. And the constraint that
decides the architecture — federal tax information cannot go to a public
endpoint; the model comes to the data.

== From data to judgement — and who climbs each step

`Rung: data · information · knowledge · judgement`

Every step up is worth more to the mission. AI moves the work up the ladder —
it never takes the top step.

[cols="1,2,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| Rung | What it is | Looks like | Who climbs to it

| *Data* (data) | What happened, uninterpreted | returns · transcripts · calls · scans | —
| *Information* (information) | Data in context for one case | matched · flagged · routed by intent | Predictive AI — score · match · flag
| *Knowledge* (knowledge) | Information that informs an outcome | guidance · precedent · taxonomies | Generative AI + RAG — read · ground · draft
| *Judgement* (judgement) | A determination someone signs | the decision — and the accountability | People — decide and sign. Stays human.
|===

*The backlog lives at the bottom of the ladder.* Transcribing, matching, routing
and summarizing consume the workforce. Every hour a machine absorbs down there is
an hour an examiner spends deciding — the only work that resolves a case.

*Judgement is deliberately never automated.* The model classifies, grounds and
drafts; a named person decides and signs. That is a design rule, not a technical
limit — and it is what makes the deployment defensible to counsel and oversight.

[.notes]
--
The masterpiece slide — Jon's word. Walk the stairs left to right: data,
information, knowledge, judgement.

Brad's line, said out loud: we are really good at creating data and information
and pretending it is knowledge — if it is not informing anything we need, it is
not knowledge. Knowledge has to drive action, not check a box.

Each rung is worth more to the mission. Predictive climbs the first riser,
generative with retrieval the second, and judgement is deliberately never
automated — that is where people come in and always will.

PLANTED QUESTION: Jon asks 'so is AI taking jobs?' — Brad answers: it is not
coming for your job, because right now it does not have judgement. The

== Predictive and generative AI answer different questions

`Rung: information · knowledge`

They fail differently — so they are governed differently.

[cols="1,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| | Predictive AI (data → information) | Generative AI (information → knowledge)

| *learns from* | Patterns in structured history — filings, payments, outcomes | Language itself — how guidance, notices and case notes are written
| *produces* | A number: a score, a forecast, a flag | Text: a classification, a draft, a summary, a cited answer
| *in tax terms* | Refund-risk scoring · workload forecasting · anomaly flags | Read correspondence · draft notices · summarize case files · answer from guidance
| *fails as* | A bad number — visible in precision and recall | A plausible sentence — which is why grounding and review are non-negotiable
| *governed by* | Back-testing, thresholds, fairness metrics | Retrieval with citations, human sign-off, drift monitoring — Labs 04 · 06
|===

[.notes]
--
Most rooms use one word — AI — for two different tools, and most failed
projects picked the wrong one. Predictive models produce a number from
structured history; generative models produce language. They fail differently,
so they are governed differently.

Close on the compose line: the strongest workflows chain them, and the platform
runs both side by side.

== Where AI actually lands in the filing lifecycle

`Rung: judgement`

Highlighted stages already have a human reviewer in the loop — which makes them
the honest places to start.

[cols="2,3,2", options="header"]
|===
| Stage | What the model does | Start here?

| 01 Intake and classification | Route by intent, not by keyword | Yes — human in the loop
| 02 Validation | Catch gaps before a case is opened | Yes — human in the loop
| 03 Correspondence | Draft; a reviewer edits and signs | Yes — human in the loop
| 04 *Exam selection* | Taxpayer determinations | *No — governance first*
| 05 Collections support | Brief the officer before the case | Yes — human in the loop
| 06 Taxpayer service | Answers carry their citation | Yes — human in the loop
|===

. *Start where a human already reviews the output* (judgement stays human). If a
  person signs the letter today, a model that drafts it changes throughput
  without changing accountability. That is a defensible first deployment.
. *Automate the toil around the model, not just the model* (executes judgement).
  Most of the effort in production AI is provisioning, patching, scaling and
  evidence collection. That is an automation problem, and it is solved.

[.notes]
--
Walk left to right. The highlighted stages are where language models earn their
keep first — high-volume, text-heavy, and a human reviewer is already in the
loop, which makes them the honest places to start.

Exam selection is deliberately NOT highlighted: that is a determination
affecting a taxpayer and needs a much heavier governance conversation — saying
so unprompted buys enormous credibility.

Two rules of thumb: start where a human already reviews the output; and
automate the toil around the model, not just the model. Worth naming: most of
the effort in production AI is provisioning, patching, scaling and data
collection — an automation problem, already solved.

== The useful pattern is a loop, not a chatbot

`Rung: information · knowledge · judgement`

A closed loop — predict, explain, ground, act, learn — each technology doing the
job it is good at. Automation closes the loop; the connected estate underneath
is the discriminator nobody else has.

[cols="1,2,3", options="header"]
|===
| Step | Technology | What happens

| *01 · Predict* — spot the pattern | Predictive ML (information) | Telemetry and historical incidents estimate risk: saturation, failure probability, anomaly or likely incident class.
| *02 · Explain* — turn signals into language | GenAI / LLM (information) | The LLM summarizes logs, changes and symptoms into a human-readable hypothesis and next questions.
| *03 · Ground + act* — use what the agency approved | RAG (knowledge) · Ansible (executes judgement) | RAG retrieves runbooks and prior fixes; policy decides whether Ansible recommends, requests approval or remediates.
| *The knowledge flywheel* | (knowledge) | After review, the incident summary, root cause, evidence and successful remediation become searchable operations knowledge for the next event. Generated content is a draft until it passes the same curation and authority rules as any other knowledge record.
|===

*Example — “Why is this service slow?”* Predictive AI says GPU queue depth is
abnormal. The LLM explains the evidence. RAG cites the approved saturation
runbook. Event-Driven Ansible scales or reroutes after the required approval.

*Boundary — do not let fluency become authority.* The LLM can propose a root
cause. Telemetry, system state, retrieved source material and deterministic
checks establish whether that explanation is actually supported.

[.notes]
--
Segue Brad rehearsed: I talk to my chatbot at night and it tells me how great I
am — but you do not want reassurance during a tax audit, a criminal
investigation, or a hunt for a pattern. It is not a chatbot. You want something
that predicts and explains, then grounds, acts and learns.

Predictive AI flags the risk from telemetry; the LLM explains the evidence; RAG
grounds it in approved runbooks; Ansible does only what policy permits, with a
human in the loop supplying knowledge and judgement. Key emphasis: the fluency
that becomes authority must be grounded in YOUR language and YOUR processes —
not OpenAI's.

Most of the effort here is provisioning, patching, scaling, data collection and
inputs — that is an automation problem, not an AI problem. Automation alone is
a differentiator; the estate — one OS, the interconnect, the whole ecosystem
with an execution layer tying it together — is the discriminator.

And generated text is a draft until it passes the same curation any knowledge
record gets.

== Ninety days, then ninety more

[cols="1,1,1", options="header"]
|===
