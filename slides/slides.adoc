== Four different jobs get called “AI”

`Rung: information · knowledge · judgement`

Treat AI as a toolbox, not a magic employee. Pick the tool by the job you need done.

[cols="1,3,2", options="header"]
|===
| Term | What it actually does | Analogy

| *Predictive AI* — estimate (data → information)
| Finds patterns in historical data to score, classify or forecast an outcome.
| A weather forecast: “there is a 70% chance,” not a guarantee.

| *Generative AI / LLM* — create (information → knowledge)
| Produces new language by predicting useful next tokens from patterns learned during training and context supplied now.
| A very well-read drafting assistant — fast, fluent, and still capable of being wrong.

| *Agentic AI* — reason + act (knowledge → action)
| Pairs a model with approved tools, state and rules so it can plan steps and call systems.
| A junior analyst with a badge and a restricted tool belt — permissions still matter.

| *Automation* — execute (executes judgement)
| Runs deterministic, tested steps when a condition or human decision says to act.
| A checklist that actually performs the checklist. This is where Ansible belongs.
|===

Predictive AI is *not* a crystal ball. An LLM is *not* a database, policy
authority or source of truth. An agent is *not* permission to bypass controls.

[.notes]
--
Start with the umbrella. Federal audiences hear AI used as if every technique
is the same. It is not. Predictive AI estimates or classifies. Generative AI
creates. An LLM is a generative model specialized in language. Agentic systems
add tools and workflow. Automation executes known steps.

The strongest mission pattern combines them rather than asking one model to do
everything.

Land the on-ramp line: if you are not automated, you are not going to have a
good generative or predictive AI inside your boundary — automation is the
prerequisite.

Jon lands the weather-forecast and well-read-drafting-assistant lines already

== The work is seasonal, textual and unforgiving

`Rung: data`

[cols="1,2,3"]
|===
| *Demand* | Peak load, fixed staff | Idle capacity in August is unjustifiable; exhausted capacity in April is a headline. A year of demand arrives in six weeks — April is 6× the August low.
| *Systems* | Rules locked in legacy code | Rewriting the systems of record is a decade. Wrapping them in intelligent workflows is a quarter.
| *Data* | The answers are in the text | Correspondence, transcripts and case notes are unstructured — exactly what language models are built for.
| *The constraint that decides everything* | The data cannot leave (data — it stays) | FTI handling rules rule out public endpoints. The model comes to the data — on premises, accredited cloud, or air-gapped enclave.
|===

[.notes]
--
Do not lead with the technology. Lead with the shape of the problem: seasonal
demand spikes against a fixed workforce, decades of correspondence logic
locked in code nobody wants to touch, and data spread across systems that were
never designed to talk.

AI is interesting here precisely because it works on unstructured language —
which is most of what a tax agency actually handles.

Context to use with tact: large federal accounts are losing institutional
knowledge as people age out or are RIF’d — capturing what the workforce knows
is part of why this matters now.

The chart: a year of demand arrives in six weeks. The analogy: Macy’s at
Thanksgiving, Black Friday and Christmas — a very seasonal workload against a
fixed store. The off-season is not waste; it is when you rewrite and integrate
the knowledge you gathered during the surge, so there is more headroom when
the next surge comes. Idle capacity in August is only unjustifiable if you do
nothing with it.

The closed loop has one rule: it feeds on data from YOUR systems of record,
added to your knowledge base to automate it — never information generated from
information. That is the snake eating its own tail.

And the constraint that decides the architecture — federal tax information
cannot go to a public endpoint; the model comes to the data.

== From data to judgement — and who climbs each step

`Rung: data · information · knowledge · judgement`

Every step up is worth more to the mission. AI moves the work up the ladder —
it never takes the top step.

[cols="1,2,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| Rung | What it is | Looks like | Who climbs to it

| *Data* (data) | What happened, uninterpreted | returns · transcripts · calls · scans | —
| *Information* (information) | Data in context for one case | matched · flagged · routed by intent | Predictive AI — score · match · flag
| *Knowledge* (knowledge) | Information that informs an outcome | guidance · precedent · taxonomies | Generative AI + RAG — read · ground · draft
| *Judgement* (judgement) | A determination someone signs | the decision — and the accountability | People — decide and sign. Stays human.
|===

*The backlog lives at the bottom of the ladder.* Transcribing, matching, routing
and summarizing consume the workforce. Every hour a machine absorbs down there is
an hour an examiner spends deciding — the only work that resolves a case.

*Judgement is deliberately never automated.* The model classifies, grounds and
drafts; a named person decides and signs. That is a design rule, not a technical
limit — and it is what makes the deployment defensible to counsel and oversight.

[.notes]
--
The masterpiece slide — Jon’s word. Say it out loud — anyone with ten or
twenty years in tech will recognize the classic
data-information-knowledge-insight progression. We replaced insight with
judgement for the AI age, and judgement is the rung that matters most: it is
the one a person signs. Walk the stairs left to right: data, information,
knowledge, judgement.

Brad’s line, said out loud: we are really good at creating data and
information and pretending it is knowledge — if it is not informing anything
we need, it is not knowledge. Knowledge has to drive action, not check a box.

Each rung is worth more to the mission. Predictive climbs the first riser,
generative with retrieval the second, and judgement is deliberately never
automated — that is where people come in and always will.

Make it concrete. Knowledge can be automated: reboot the mail server every
Friday, patch the Windows devices every Tuesday — a directive, a mandate or an
AO memo turned into a job. Judgement is knowing that a Tuesday when an AWS
GovCloud region is down is not the time to run it. AI is not replacing that
person; their call feeds back into the loop as the next piece of knowledge.

PLANTED QUESTION: Jon asks ‘so is AI taking jobs?’ — Brad answers: it is not
coming for your job, because right now it does not have judgement. The
make-the-tests-pass example: ask an AI to make all the tests pass and it edits
the code so they pass — it did exactly what you asked. That is information,
not knowledge and judgement.

Optional garnish, correctly attributed: Karpathy’s ‘you can outsource
intelligence but you cannot outsource understanding.’

== Predictive and generative AI answer different questions

`Rung: information · knowledge`

They fail differently — so they are governed differently.

[cols="1,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| | Predictive AI (data → information) | Generative AI (information → knowledge)

| *learns from* | Patterns in structured history — filings, payments, outcomes | Language itself — how guidance, notices and case notes are written
| *produces* | A number: a score, a forecast, a flag | Text: a classification, a draft, a summary, a cited answer
| *in tax terms* | Refund-risk scoring · workload forecasting · anomaly flags | Read correspondence · draft notices · summarize case files · answer from guidance
| *fails as* | A bad number — visible in precision and recall | A plausible sentence — which is why grounding and review are non-negotiable
| *governed by* | Back-testing, thresholds, fairness metrics | Retrieval with citations, human sign-off, drift monitoring — Labs 04 · 06
|===

overlap where a score picks the case and a draft explains it, and a person
decides.

== Where AI actually lands in the filing lifecycle

`Rung: judgement`

AI lands on the first four stages, where a reviewer already checks the output;
the decision stays with a person; operations close the loop.

[cols="1,3,5,3", options="header"]
|===
| # | Station | What happens | Who acts

| 01 | Intake | Capture correspondence from any channel | AI reads it
| 02 | Classification | Understand intent, route to the case type | AI classifies
| 03 | Guidance | Rules, policy and precedent | AI grounds and cites
| 04 | Drafting | Letters, responses, memos, with citations | AI drafts
| 05 | Review | Check accuracy, add judgment, approve | Human in the loop
| 06 | Decision | Finalize and issue the outcome | A person signs
| 07 | Notice | Send letters and update records | Automation executes
| 08 | Operations | Track performance, learn and improve | The loop closes
|===

. *Start where a human already reviews the output* (judgement stays human). If a
  person signs the letter today, a model that drafts it changes throughput
  without changing accountability. That is a defensible first deployment.
. *Automate the toil around the model, not just the model* (executes judgement).
  Most of the effort in production AI is provisioning, patching, scaling and
  evidence collection. That is an automation problem, and it is solved.

[.notes]
--
Walk the eight stations left to right, the same eight as the graphic (G): AI
reads, classifies, grounds and drafts on the first four; a person reviews and
signs on five and six; automation sends the notice; operations close the loop.
The first four are where language models earn their keep first — high-volume,
text-heavy, and a human reviewer is already in the loop, which makes them the
honest places to start.

Exam selection is deliberately NOT on this rail (an earlier version of the
slide showed it held back): that is a determination affecting a taxpayer and
needs a much heavier governance conversation — saying so unprompted buys
enormous credibility.

Two rules of thumb: start where a human already reviews the output; and
automate the toil around the model, not just the model. Worth naming: most of
the effort in production AI is provisioning, patching, scaling and data
collection — an automation problem, already solved.

GRAPHIC (G) — Where AI actually lands in the filing lifecycle: An eight-
station rail, Intake, Classification, Guidance, Drafting, Review, Decision,
Notice and Operations, with five red call-outs over the first five stations;
notice that the fifth call-out is a human reviewer and Decision has no call-
out at all. Talk to it: (1) Read the rail against the ladder: Intake through
Drafting turn data into information and knowledge; Review and Decision are
where a person supplies the judgement that stays human. (2) The four AI cards
sit where a reviewer already checks the output; neither this graphic nor the
slide puts AI on exam selection, because a taxpayer determination needs
governance first. (3) Operations closes the loop: what Review corrects and
Notice sends goes back into the knowledge base between seasons, and pooled
GPUs absorb the 6× April surge on a fixed workforce. (4) One platform under
all eight stations: the model comes to the data, the seams stay boring, and
controls accredited once for Intake are inherited all the way to Operations.

Inference — serve efficiently with vLLM / llm-d · Data — connect private agency

[cols="1,2,4", options="header"]
|===

| Choice | *Model freedom* (knowledge) | Open models and standard APIs keep the exit open. Modern lock-in limits what you can build next — a worse trap than the old hardware kind.

== The useful pattern is a loop, not a chatbot

`Rung: information · knowledge · judgement`

Predict, explain, act: spot the pattern, turn the signals into language, use
only what the agency has approved. Grounding and learning are how the loop earns
the right to act; the connected estate underneath is the discriminator nobody
else has.

[cols="1,2,3", options="header"]
|===
| Step | Technology | What happens

| *01 · Predict* — spot the pattern | Predictive ML (information) | Telemetry and historical incidents estimate risk: saturation, failure probability, anomaly or likely incident class.
| *02 · Explain* — turn signals into language | GenAI / LLM (information) | The LLM summarizes logs, changes and symptoms into a human-readable hypothesis and next questions.
| *03 · Ground + act* — use what the agency approved | RAG (knowledge) · Ansible (executes judgement) | RAG retrieves runbooks and prior fixes; policy decides whether Ansible recommends, requests approval or remediates. Every action opens and closes an ITSM ticket (ServiceNow, Remedy), so the incident record is the trail. Lab 05.
| *The knowledge flywheel* | (knowledge) | After review, the incident summary, root cause, evidence and successful remediation become searchable operations knowledge for the next event. Generated content is a draft until it passes the same curation and authority rules as any other knowledge record.
|===

*Example — “Why is this service slow?”* Predictive AI says GPU queue depth is
abnormal. The LLM explains the evidence. RAG cites the approved saturation
runbook. Event-Driven Ansible scales or reroutes after the required approval.

*Boundary — do not let fluency become authority.* The LLM can propose a root
cause. Telemetry, system state, retrieved source material and deterministic
checks establish whether that explanation is actually supported.

[.notes]
--
Segue Brad rehearsed: I talk to my chatbot at night and it tells me how great
I am — but you do not want reassurance during a tax audit, a criminal
investigation, or a hunt for a pattern. It is not a chatbot. You want
something that predicts and explains, then grounds, acts and learns.

Lead with the triad: predict, explain, act. You cannot act unless you have
explained, and you cannot explain unless you have predicted. The patterns
already exist in your ITSM — P1 through P5 severity and mean-time-to-restore
records are what you organize into signals.

Predictive AI flags the risk from telemetry; the LLM explains the evidence;
RAG grounds it in approved runbooks; Ansible does only what policy permits,
with a human in the loop supplying knowledge and judgement.

At ACT: every action opens and closes a ticket in the ITSM — ServiceNow,
Remedy, whatever you run — so you keep track for incident management purposes;
that ticket trail is the closed loop system of record.

Caution: do not say agentless or serviceless as an absolute in front of cyber
— say no persistent agent, transport is SSH. Ansible is technically not
serviceless automation; we speak in general terms, and one SSH-is-a-service
objection can derail the loop.

At LEARN: what the loop learns from is data from your own systems — never
information generated from information.

Key emphasis: the fluency that becomes authority must be grounded in YOUR
language and YOUR processes — not OpenAI’s.

Most of the effort here is provisioning, patching, scaling, data collection
and inputs — that is an automation problem, not an AI problem. Automation
alone is a differentiator; the estate — one OS, the interconnect, the whole
ecosystem with an execution layer tying it together — is the discriminator.

And generated text is a draft until it passes the same curation any knowledge
